No. 010 — 2026-10-01 — freelance, money

Reverse-engineering your hourly rate from real numbers

Most freelance pricing advice stops at the motivational-poster stage. "Know your value." Helpful. Here is the math that actually gets you to a number.

Start with what you need to take home

Pick a target annual net income — not revenue, not what you'd like to brag about, but what needs to land in your bank account after taxes to cover your life. Be literal: rent, food, insurance, debt payments, savings rate, all of it. Add those up for a year. Call this number Net.

Now gross it up for taxes. If you're self-employed in the US, you're covering both sides of payroll tax plus income tax, which for most people in a middle income range means you should divide Net by roughly 0.65 to 0.70 to get your required gross revenue. (Your actual rate depends on your situation — run it by a tax person.) Call this Required Revenue.

Account for the hours you can actually bill

A full-time job is roughly 2,000 hours a year. You will not bill 2,000 hours as a freelancer. You will spend real, unrecoverable hours on business development, invoicing, client emails, proposals that go nowhere, and the occasional staring-at-the-wall hour.

A conservative estimate for a solo freelancer: 50% of working hours are billable. That's 1,000 hours. Some people run higher once established, some run lower when they're starting out or in a slow patch. Pick a number between 800 and 1,200 that reflects your actual situation, not your optimistic one. Call this Billable Hours.

Your floor rate is simply:

Required Revenue ÷ Billable Hours = Minimum Hourly Rate

If you need $70,000 to hit your net income target, that grosses up to roughly $105,000 in required revenue. Divide by 1,000 billable hours: your floor is $105/hr. Below that number, you are slowly going broke while feeling busy.

Add your real business costs

The calculation above covers your income. It does not cover the cost of running the business. Health insurance, software subscriptions, equipment, professional development, accountant fees, liability insurance if you carry it — these need to come out of revenue too, before you pay yourself.

Add up your annual business expenses. Call this Overhead. Redo the math:

(Required Revenue + Overhead) ÷ Billable Hours = Actual Floor Rate

A lot of freelancers discover at this step that they've been undercharging by $15 to $30 an hour, because they'd been eating business costs out of what they thought was their salary.

Build in a buffer for dry spells

The floor rate assumes you hit your billable hours target every month. You won't. Some months you'll bill 90 hours, some months 40. The rate you charge during good months needs to subsidize the slow ones.

One approach: assume you'll only work at your target utilization rate for 10 months out of 12. Recalculate with 10/12 of your billable hours in the denominator. This builds a cushion without requiring you to separately forecast every slow month.

What the market wants is a separate conversation

Everything above gives you your floor — the number below which you cannot sustainably operate. The ceiling is what clients in your specific market will pay for the specific value you provide. Both numbers matter.

If your floor is $110/hr and the market for your skill in your niche runs $75-$95/hr, that is real information. It means your cost structure is too high, your target income is too high for your current positioning, or you need to move up-market. "Charge what you're worth" doesn't resolve this; the math at least shows you exactly where the gap is and which variable to work on.

If your floor is $80/hr and strong practitioners in your niche regularly get $150/hr, you have room to move up. Don't anchor on your floor as your rate — anchor on the floor as your walk-away number.

Try this

Open a spreadsheet or grab a piece of paper. Write down your target annual net income. Gross it up by dividing by 0.67. Add your estimated annual business expenses. Divide the total by 1,000 (or whatever billable-hour estimate feels honest for your situation). That number is your floor rate. Compare it to what you're currently charging. If there's a gap, write down which variable — income target, tax load, overhead, or billable hours — is most within your control to change.

The point isn't to find the perfect number in 15 minutes. It's to stop operating on intuition when arithmetic is available.

Field note

If this is a recurring problem for you, it's usually worth having a time-tracking or invoicing tool on hand rather than solving it from scratch each time.
