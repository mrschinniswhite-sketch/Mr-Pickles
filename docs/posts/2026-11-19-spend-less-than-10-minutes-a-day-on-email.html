Spend less than 10 minutes a day on email

Most people treat their inbox as a to-do list someone else controls. The result is a workday organized around other people's urgency, which is usually not your urgency.

The basic premise

Triage means sorting, not responding. The goal of a 10-minute session is to decide what each item is, not to handle it. You are a dispatcher, not a first responder. Once you internalize that distinction, the whole thing gets faster.

Do this twice a day—once in the morning, once in the late afternoon. Close the inbox between sessions. That part is non-negotiable; otherwise you're back to ambient monitoring, which is the problem you're trying to solve.

The four buckets

Every email is one of four things:

Trash. Newsletters you don't read, automated notifications you can get elsewhere, anything that doesn't require your attention at all. Delete or archive immediately. Don't open them fully. Subject line is enough.

Reference. You might need this later but there's no action on your end. File it. If your email client supports archiving with a keyboard shortcut, use it. This should take under two seconds per message.

Waiting. You've sent something and are expecting a reply, or someone else needs to do something before you can proceed. Some people keep a dedicated label or folder for this; others track it in a separate list. The inbox is not a waiting room.

Action. Something that actually requires you to do something. If that something takes under two minutes and you're not in a focused work block, do it now. If it takes longer, move it to your actual task system—a separate app, a written list, whatever you use—and get it out of the inbox.

The trap is letting "action" items sit in your inbox with the vague intention of handling them later. They become visual noise that makes you re-read and re-decide every time you open the inbox.

Keeping the session under 10 minutes

Speed comes from decision hygiene, not speed-reading. A few things that help:

Don't open an email unless the subject line is ambiguous. Most of the time you can sort by subject line alone. When you do open one, make the decision before you close it. Reopening the same message later is where time disappears.

Unsubscribe ruthlessly during the first week. It costs 20 seconds now and saves you that decision every day for the rest of your life. After about two weeks of active unsubscribing, your incoming volume will be noticeably lower.

Batch your context. Sort by sender or subject if you have many related messages. Making ten decisions in the same category is faster than ten decisions across ten different categories.

What breaks this system

Checking email out of habit between sessions. This is usually not about urgency—it's boredom, anxiety, or a reflexive reach for stimulation. Turning off push notifications and removing the app from your phone's most accessible screen helps more than any inbox system will.

Also: not having a real task system to receive the action items. If your only option is "leave it in the inbox or lose it," you will leave it in the inbox. The email triage system only works if it has somewhere to put things.

Try this

Open your inbox right now and set a timer for 10 minutes. Go through every message you can in that time using only the four buckets: delete, archive, waiting, or move to a task list. Don't respond to anything that takes more than two minutes. When the timer goes off, stop—even if there are messages left. The point isn't to finish; it's to see how the sorting feels when you're not also trying to respond, think, and plan at the same time. Most people find they can process two or three times as many messages as usual.

Closing

The inbox will still have things in it. That's fine. The goal is not inbox zero as a daily achievement—it's stopping the inbox from functioning as your default attention manager. Triage gives you a brief, bounded interaction with email twice a day, and then you close it and go do work. That's the whole system.

Field note

If this is a recurring problem for you, it's usually worth having an email client or inbox tool on hand rather than solving it from scratch each time.
