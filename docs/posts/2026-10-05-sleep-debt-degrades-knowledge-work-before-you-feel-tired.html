No. 011 — 2026-10-05 — wellbeing, focus

Sleep debt degrades knowledge work before you feel tired

Most people wait for the heavy eyelids or the afternoon slump to tell them their sleep debt is real. By then, the morning's work has already been quietly undermined.

The editing instinct goes first

The clearest early signal is a drop in self-editing. You write something and it reads fine to you. You send an email that's slightly off in tone, or a document with a logic gap you'd normally catch on a read-through. The problem isn't that you can't produce words — it's that the internal reviewer who normally runs a half-second check on everything you output has gone quiet. This is why sleep-deprived writing often looks plausible on the surface and only reveals its problems to a fresh reader. You can draft fluently and still be operating impaired.

Decision fatigue arrives earlier in the day

On a normal day, the capacity to make decent decisions degrades gradually over hours of use. After poor sleep, you start closer to the depleted end. This shows up practically as an increased tendency to defer, to go with defaults, to avoid any choice that requires comparing options. You'll notice it in small ways — staring at a menu of tasks and picking the most obvious one repeatedly, or leaving genuinely important decisions as "let me think about that later" three times in a row. Later never arrives with a better answer, because the depletion doesn't repair itself mid-day.

Switching cost goes up significantly

Knowledge work requires moving between contexts constantly — you're in a spreadsheet, then a document, then a conversation, then back to the spreadsheet. The mental overhead of each switch is normally modest. After poor sleep, it's not. Re-entry into a task takes longer. You spend more time re-reading your own notes to remember where you were, more time just sitting before the work actually starts again. Focused blocks feel shorter and less useful than usual, not because you're distracted by something else, but because a larger fraction of each block is spent on overhead. The work session happens, but more of it is staging and less of it is progress.

Novelty tolerance collapses

This one is easy to miss as a sleep symptom because it can look like good judgment. When you're under-rested, genuinely new problems — ones that don't have an obvious precedent to reach for — feel disproportionately effortful. You'll find yourself reaching for familiar templates, solutions you've used before, or responses that fit broadly even if they don't fit precisely. Creative synthesis, the kind that requires holding two unrelated things in your head and finding the connection, is essentially the first higher-order function to degrade. If you're a person whose work involves finding non-obvious angles, poor sleep doesn't make you wrong — it makes you more conventional, which can be just as costly.

Your tolerance for ambiguity shrinks

A related but distinct effect: tasks that lack a clear endpoint become intolerable faster. Under-rested, you push for premature closure on problems that actually need more time to sit. You'll arrive at a "good enough" answer and stop looking, not because you've assessed that it's sufficient but because the state of not-yet-knowing has become uncomfortable enough to resolve at any cost. In writing, this shows up as conclusions that are too tidy. In analysis, it's the interpretation that fits the data well enough and doesn't get challenged. In client work, it's the proposal you sent a little too soon.

Try this

Pick a piece of knowledge work you completed in the last week — a document, an analysis, a substantive email, anything that took real thought. Read it now with the specific question: where did I stop short. Look for places where a vague word replaced a precise one, where a claim was made but not supported, where a conclusion was reached before the reasoning was finished. Note how many you find. Then identify what your sleep looked like in the 48 hours before you wrote it. Do this three or four times over the following weeks. You'll start to build a calibration for what your own work looks like when you're rested versus when you're not — which is more useful than any general description of sleep deprivation, because you're measuring the specific ways your particular output degrades.

The goal isn't to optimize sleep as a productivity hack. It's to be accurate about what you're actually producing. Tired work that feels competent while you're doing it is the hardest kind to catch, and catching it before it leaves your desk is considerably easier than catching it afterward.

Field note

If this is a recurring problem for you, it's usually worth having a sleep tracker or blue-light glasses on hand rather than solving it from scratch each time.
