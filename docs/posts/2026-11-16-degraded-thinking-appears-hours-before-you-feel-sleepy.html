No. 023 — 2026-11-16 — wellbeing, focus

Degraded thinking appears hours before you feel sleepy

Most people wait until they feel exhausted to treat sleep as a problem. By then, they've already spent several days producing work that required more time than it should have and contained more errors than they'd like to admit.

The first casualty is editing judgment

Good writing, analysis, and code review all require the same underlying skill: the ability to look at something you just made and recognize that it isn't quite right. This metacognitive layer — the part that says "this argument doesn't hold, revise it" — degrades before you feel slow. You can still produce output at something close to your normal rate. You just can't evaluate it accurately. The result is work that feels finished but isn't, submitted with more confidence than it deserves.

This is why sleep-deprived knowledge workers often don't notice their own impairment. The monitoring function is the first thing that goes offline.

Decisions get quietly worse

Decision quality deteriorates in a specific pattern under poor sleep, not uniformly. The first thing to go is consideration of downside risk. Tired people tend toward whichever option requires less mental effort to justify, then construct reasoning around it after the fact. If you notice yourself thinking "good enough" faster than usual, or feeling mild irritation when someone raises a complication, that's signal worth taking seriously.

The other tell is that your decision horizon shrinks. You're more likely to choose the thing that solves today's problem rather than the thing that's actually correct across time. This shows up in solo work as deferred maintenance — skipping the refactor, letting the filing system slide, choosing the quick client response over the accurate one.

Reading becomes shallow

Comprehension of dense material requires holding multiple things in working memory simultaneously while also tracking how they relate. Under poor sleep, working memory capacity drops noticeably before sustained attention does. You can sit and read for the same amount of time, but you retain and connect less.

The practical sign is re-reading. If you find yourself finishing a paragraph and immediately returning to the top of it, not because it's poorly written but because nothing stuck, that's a working-memory failure. It tends to precede the heaviness behind the eyes by several hours.

Estimation gets optimistic in the wrong direction

Tired people chronically underestimate how long tasks will take. Partly this is cognitive — you're not modeling the full complexity of the task accurately. Partly it's motivational — if you face the true scope of what something requires, it feels overwhelming, so you don't. The result is over-commitment and then a cascade of small failures of follow-through that feel like discipline problems but are physiological.

Planning work done on poor sleep — weekly reviews, project scoping, any place where you're assessing how much you can handle — tends to be the most unreliable. The confidence feels the same. The accuracy isn't.

The social and communication layer

For anyone whose work involves other people, even asynchronously, the communication output is usually where degraded sleep is most visible to others before it's visible to you. Emails get longer and less clear. The right word doesn't come and you work around it in a way that obscures what you mean. Tone calibration fails — messages come across as blunter or more anxious than intended because you're not running the usual pass where you check how something lands.

If you go back and reread things you wrote during a run of bad nights, they're often technically coherent but slightly off in ways that are hard to name. They sound like you, but a version of you that's slightly less careful.

Try this

Pick a morning when you've had genuinely good sleep and a morning when you haven't. On each of those mornings, spend eight minutes writing a short explanation of a decision you're currently sitting with — why you're inclined toward one option, what the risks are, what you might be missing. Don't compare them immediately. Wait a few days, then read both cold. The difference in how much you actually interrogated your reasoning — not how the prose sounds, but how hard you pushed on your own assumptions — is usually obvious. Do this once and you'll have a reliable, firsthand calibration tool that tells you more than any sleep score app.

Poor sleep doesn't feel like impairment from the inside. It feels like a slightly harder day. The gap between those two descriptions is where the real output cost lives.

Field note

If this is a recurring problem for you, it's usually worth having a sleep tracker or blue-light glasses on hand rather than solving it from scratch each time.
