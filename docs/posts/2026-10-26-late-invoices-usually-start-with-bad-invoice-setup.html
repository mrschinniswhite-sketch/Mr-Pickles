Late invoices usually start with bad invoice setup

Most late payments are not the client's fault. That's an uncomfortable thing to admit, but it's usually true — the invoice had a problem, and the problem was upstream of the client ever seeing it.

The invoice that disappears into a queue

When you send an invoice to a generic address — billing@, accounts@, finance@ — it lands in a queue managed by someone who has never spoken to you. That person's job is to process paperwork, not advocate for you. If anything is missing, unclear, or doesn't match what's in their system, the path of least resistance is to set it aside. Not reject it. Just set it aside. You won't hear anything. The invoice will age quietly while you assume it's being processed.

The fix is boring but effective: find out before you start the project who handles vendor payments, what information they need on an invoice, and what purchase order number or reference code to include. One email to your day-to-day contact at the start of the engagement gets you this. Most freelancers never send that email.

The terms no one agreed to

"Net 30" typed at the bottom of an invoice is not a payment agreement. It's a note. If you never discussed payment terms during the project negotiation, your client may be operating on their standard terms — which might be Net 45, Net 60, or "whenever the next check run is." You invoicing with different terms doesn't override that.

Agree on payment terms in writing before the work starts. This does not have to be a formal contract. An email that says "I invoice at project completion, with payment due within 14 days — does that work?" and gets a reply that says "yes" is sufficient. Now you both have the same number in your heads, and a late payment is clearly late.

The invoice that's hard to pay

Clients pay invoices in batches, usually on a schedule their accounting department sets. If your invoice requires them to do something unusual — call you to get your bank details, write a check when they prefer ACH, use a payment portal they haven't registered for — it will wait until someone has time to deal with it. That time may not come for weeks.

Put your preferred payment method information directly on the invoice. All of it, without the client needing to ask. If you accept bank transfer, include the routing details. If you use an invoicing platform that generates a pay-now link, make sure that link is prominent. Reduce the number of steps between "client opens invoice" and "client pays invoice" to as few as possible.

The invoice sent at the wrong moment

Friday afternoon invoices get pushed to Monday. Monday invoices compete with the week's new priorities. Invoices sent while your contact is on vacation sit until they return, then sit a little longer while they catch up. None of this is malicious.

Send invoices on Tuesday or Wednesday mornings. Invoice immediately when work is delivered, not at the end of the month as part of a batch — the work is freshest in the client's mind right after delivery, which is when they're most motivated to close it out. If you're going to miss a billing cycle, invoice anyway and note it.

Try this

Pull up your last three invoices — the documents themselves, not your records of them. Check each one for: a specific named contact (not a generic address), a PO or reference number if the client uses them, complete payment instructions, and agreed terms you can point to in an email thread. If any of those four elements is missing from any invoice, draft the one follow-up email or add the one piece of information that would have filled the gap. Do this before you send your next invoice.

Following up without feeling awkward

A lot of freelancers let invoices run past due because they dread the follow-up. The follow-up is much easier when you have a paper trail. "Following up on invoice #47, sent March 4, due March 18 per our agreement" is a simple factual statement. It's not a confrontation. It's a reminder. Send it the day after the due date, not two weeks later when you're frustrated.

The goal is to make payment the easy, obvious next step at every point in the process. Most clients are not trying to avoid paying you — they're busy, and their systems are imperfect. An invoice that arrives with all the right information, addressed to the right person, with clear terms and clear instructions, is one that gets processed. An invoice with gaps in it is one that waits.

Field note

If this is a recurring problem for you, it's usually worth having an invoicing or bookkeeping tool on hand rather than solving it from scratch each time.
