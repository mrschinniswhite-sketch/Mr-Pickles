No. 016 — 2026-10-22 — focus

Phones made focus harder. Close the door anyway.

Distraction has always been cheap, but it has gotten structurally cheaper. The apps competing for your attention in 2025 are not the same apps from 2019 — they have had five more years of engineering poured into the specific problem of making you look at them.

What changed, and it wasn't you

The usual explanation for why you can't focus is a personal failing: you're undisciplined, you lack willpower, you need a better morning routine. That framing is convenient for the people building the tools you're distracted by.

What actually changed is the interrupt model. Five years ago, most notifications were pull-based — you went and checked things. The drift since then has been toward push, toward ambient presence, toward apps that have legitimate reasons to surface themselves at any moment. Your calendar pings you. Your project management tool pings you when someone comments. Your communication tool pings you, then pings you again with a summary of what you missed while ignoring the first ping. The individual interrupts feel reasonable. The cumulative architecture does not.

There's also been a meaningful shift in what "open a browser" means. In 2019, the browser was a place you went to do something. It has since become a live feed that follows you everywhere, with news tickers, AI chat sidebars, and browser homepages that have been optimized to keep you there. The friction between "I need to look something up" and "I have now been reading sideways for eleven minutes" has dropped to nearly zero.

Why single-tasking fights you now

The standard advice — turn off notifications, use a focus mode — is not wrong, but it treats the symptom. The structural problem is that all of your tools are connected to the same network, running in the same environment, and most of them were not designed with your concentration as the primary goal. They were designed to be useful enough that you keep them open.

Single-tasking used to be the default. You sat at a desk with paper, then later a computer that ran one program visibly at a time. Context-switching was slow and physical. Now it is one keystroke or one glance at a phone, and the habit has been trained into reflexes most people don't notice firing.

One constraint that actually works: the physical separation

The most reliable forcing function for single-tasking is not a software setting but a physical one. Use a second device — or a deliberately crippled version of your main device — that contains only what you need for the work you're doing right now.

This sounds like overkill until you try it. An old laptop with no email client and a browser profile with no saved passwords for social or news sites is extraordinarily boring in exactly the right way. You open it and there is essentially nothing to do except the thing you brought it for. The boredom is the point. Your brain, finding no easy exits, eventually settles.

Phones work well for this in reverse: leaving yours in another room is a well-worn suggestion, but specifically leaving it face-down somewhere inconvenient — not a drawer, not a pocket, but a place that requires you to stand up and walk — adds just enough friction that the reflex-check becomes a conscious choice.

The mechanism here is that you are not relying on willpower to resist the pull. You are changing the physical layout so the pull has nowhere to land.

Try this

Pick one task that requires at least 45 minutes of continuous thinking — writing, planning, something you've been avoiding. Before you start, close every application on your computer that is not directly required for that task. Not minimize. Close. Then log out of your browser entirely, so that reopening it requires you to type a password. Set a paper or basic digital timer for 45 minutes and start. Notice at what point the first urge to switch fires, and what triggered it. You don't have to write it down, but notice it. That moment — the first itch — is the thing you're training against.

What this changes over time

The goal is not a heroic unbroken focus session. It's learning where your specific leaks are, and making them slightly more expensive to indulge. The environment has moved against single-tasking in ways that are real and will probably continue. The response that works is not discipline in the motivational-poster sense, but friction — deliberate, physical, boring friction placed between you and the thing that pulls you sideways. It doesn't have to be elegant to work.

Field note

If this is a recurring problem for you, it's usually worth having a distraction-free writing tool on hand rather than solving it from scratch each time.
