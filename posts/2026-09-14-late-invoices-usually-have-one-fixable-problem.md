Most late payments are not the client's fault. That's an uncomfortable thing to admit, but the invoice itself is usually where the delay starts.

## What a client actually does with your invoice

When an invoice lands in someone's inbox, it goes through a small gauntlet before anyone pays it. Someone has to match it to a project or purchase order, confirm the amount is what they expected, figure out who needs to approve it, and then route it to whoever actually processes payments. If any of those steps stalls — because something is missing, confusing, or surprising — the invoice sits. It will sit for days, sometimes weeks, until someone has the bandwidth to chase down the missing piece. That someone is usually not you.

## The mistakes that cause the stall

**No purchase order or reference number.** Many companies, especially larger ones, cannot process an invoice that doesn't reference a PO number. If you didn't get one before starting work, ask for it before sending the invoice. If the client says they don't use them, note that on the invoice explicitly so it doesn't get kicked back by their accounts payable team.

**Vague line items.** "Consulting services — October" tells a client nothing they can verify. "Copy editing, annual report draft (three rounds, October 3–18)" tells them exactly what they agreed to pay for. The more your line item matches how your client described the project internally, the easier it is to approve without questions.

**Invoice sent to the wrong person.** You may have a warm relationship with your day-to-day contact, but they are often not the person who processes payments. Ask, once, at the start of every new client relationship: "Who should invoices go to, and is there anything your billing process requires?" Most people are happy to tell you.

**No clear due date.** "Net 30" is common but it's also easy to deprioritize. A specific date — "Payment due November 14" — is harder to mentally defer. Some clients will still pay on their own schedule regardless, but a concrete date removes any ambiguity that lets them push it to next week.

**Invoicing late in the billing cycle.** If a client runs payroll or vendor payments on a specific schedule, an invoice that arrives a day after cutoff waits until the next cycle. You may never know the cutoff exists until you ask.

## The real problem underneath all of this

Each of the above mistakes is essentially the same mistake: the invoice requires the client to do extra work. They have to fill in missing information, chase someone to ask a question, or make a judgment call about something that should have been resolved earlier. Every bit of friction you add is a reason for the invoice to move to the bottom of the pile. The fix isn't chasing harder after you've sent a bad invoice. It's reducing friction before you send.

## Try this

Take your current invoice template and read it as if you were a new accounts payable clerk who has never heard of you or your project. Ask yourself three questions: Do I know exactly what was delivered and when? Do I know who authorized this? Do I know when it needs to be paid and how? If any answer is no, add the missing information. This takes about ten minutes and will improve your payment speed more than any follow-up email ever will.

## The process change that handles most of it

Send a "pre-invoice" confirmation before you invoice. At project completion — or a week before your scheduled invoice date for retainer work — send a one-sentence message to your contact: "I'll be invoicing $X for [project] on [date], routed to [billing contact]. Let me know if anything looks off before then." This does three things. It gives the client a chance to surface problems before the invoice is in the system, it confirms your billing contact is still correct, and it creates a paper trail showing the amount was expected. Clients who might have been surprised by the invoice now have a moment to raise concerns or approve the number internally before it arrives. Surprised clients slow-pay. Unsurprised clients generally don't.

None of this requires new software or a different billing service. It requires treating the invoice as a document your client has to act on, not just a formality you have to send.
