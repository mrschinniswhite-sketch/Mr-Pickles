Most freelance rate advice stops at "know your value" and then hands you a vague confidence pep talk. That's not useful. What's useful is working backward from a real number.

## Start with what you actually need to take home

Pick an annual net income target — not revenue, not gross, but what lands in your account after taxes. Be honest about taxes; depending on your situation and location, self-employment tax alone can take a significant bite before income tax even enters the picture. A rough working assumption for many solo professionals in the US is to set aside somewhere between 25–35% of gross for taxes, but check with an accountant for your actual situation.

So if you need $60,000 net, and you're estimating 30% going to taxes, you need to gross roughly $86,000. That's your revenue floor before you've bought a single software subscription or paid for health insurance.

## Add your actual business costs

Now list what you spend to operate: software tools, professional development, home office costs, health insurance premiums, liability insurance, any subcontractors, accounting fees. Don't guess — open your bank statements and add it up. For many solo professionals this runs anywhere from a few thousand to $20,000 or more annually depending on the field.

Add that number to your revenue floor. If your costs are $12,000 a year, your target revenue is now $98,000. That's the number your hourly rate has to generate.

## Count only billable hours, not working hours

This is where most freelancers underestimate. You do not bill for 40 hours a week, 52 weeks a year. You have unpaid time for sales calls, proposals, invoicing, email, marketing, professional development, sick days, and vacation. A realistic billable-hour count for a solo freelancer working full-time is often somewhere between 900 and 1,200 hours annually — not the 2,080 hours a full-time employee clocks.

Divide your target revenue by your realistic billable hours. At $98,000 needed and 1,000 billable hours:

$98,000 ÷ 1,000 = **$98/hour**

That's your floor rate — the number below which you're subsidizing your clients' businesses with your own financial stability. Some people look at that number and think it's too high to charge. Usually that feeling is worth examining rather than immediately acting on.

## Factor in your utilization reality

New freelancers especially tend to assume they'll be fully booked. They won't be, at first. If you think you'll realistically bill 60% of your available hours in year one, you can't just price for 100% utilization and hope it works out. Either increase your rate to cover the gap, reduce your income target temporarily, cut costs, or some combination. The math doesn't negotiate.

A useful sanity check: take the rate you calculated and multiply it by the hours you're actually likely to bill in the next 90 days based on current client workload. Does that quarterly number put you on track for your annual revenue target? If not, something has to change — and it's usually the rate, not some optimistic projection about future bookings.

## What the market rate conversation actually means

Market rates matter, but not in the way people use them to feel better about undercharging. Knowing what others in your field charge tells you whether your positioning and service packaging need adjustment, not whether your costs are negotiable. Your rent does not care what the going rate for junior copywriters is.

If your floor rate is significantly above what clients in your current market are paying, that's real information — but the solution isn't to price below your floor. It's to either move up-market, specialize more narrowly, productize your services differently, or reduce your cost structure. Pricing below your floor rate isn't a business strategy; it's a slow exit from freelancing.

## Try this

Open a spreadsheet and do the calculation once, with your actual numbers. Enter your desired net income, estimate your effective tax rate (err on the high side), add your real annual business expenses from the last 12 months of statements, and divide the total by 1,000 hours. Whatever number comes out is your floor. Write it somewhere visible. You don't have to charge exactly that rate tomorrow, but you should know what it is before you quote your next project.

The goal here isn't to arrive at a number that feels comfortable. It's to stop making pricing decisions in the dark.
