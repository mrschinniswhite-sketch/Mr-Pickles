Being easy to work with on an async team is not a personality trait. It is a set of behaviors, most of which can be learned in an afternoon and refined over a few months.

## Write to be read once

The single biggest drain on async teams is the message that requires follow-up questions. If someone has to reply "which project?" or "what's the deadline on this?" you've just added anywhere from two hours to two days of lag to a simple exchange. Before you send anything, read it as if you've never seen the context. Name the project. Name the file. Specify the date with the year. Include the link instead of describing where the link lives. This sounds pedantic until you're the person waiting on the other end of a vague Slack message at 11pm in a different timezone.

The same principle applies to longer documents. Put the ask or the conclusion at the top, not the bottom. People read async messages when they have a window, which is often a short one. Don't make them scroll to find out what you need from them.

## Respect the timezone math without making it a performance

You don't need to constantly announce that your teammates are in different timezones. You need to quietly account for it. When you schedule something, include the timezone explicitly. When you're setting a deadline, be honest with yourself about what "end of day" means when your team spans twelve hours. When you need a decision before you can move forward, say so early enough that the lag doesn't become your team's problem.

Async-friendly people also get comfortable with the idea that they may have to make a reasonable judgment call and move forward, leaving a clear note about what they decided and why. Waiting on approval for every small thing when the approver is asleep is a choice to stall. Document your reasoning and keep moving on low-stakes decisions.

## Default to over-documentation, but make it findable

Good async teammates leave a trail. Not a rambling stream-of-consciousness trail, but a useful one. When you make a decision, write down why. When a plan changes, update the relevant document and note the change. When you finish a piece of work, leave a short summary of what you did and what someone picking it up later would need to know.

The failure mode here is documentation that exists but can't be found. A note buried in a thread from four months ago is not documentation. It's archaeology. Put important information where people will actually look for it, and use whatever naming conventions your team has agreed on. If your team has no naming conventions, propose one. It doesn't have to be perfect.

## Signal your availability without being precious about it

Async does not mean perpetually available. It also doesn't mean you vanish without a trace. Update your status when you're heads-down or offline. If you're going to be unreachable for longer than usual, say so and say when you'll be back. This is not about asking permission to have a life. It's about giving your teammates accurate information so they can route around you when necessary without anxiety.

On the flip side, if you tend toward over-communication about your schedule ("just a heads up I'm going to grab lunch, back in 45"), dial it back. The signal matters; the ceremony around it does not.

## Try this

Take the last three messages or tasks you handed off to a teammate and read them with fresh eyes. For each one, ask: could someone act on this without asking me a single follow-up question? Note every place where you used a vague reference (the doc, that project, soon, ASAP) and rewrite just those phrases with specific ones. Takes about ten minutes, and it will show you your own patterns faster than any amount of abstract advice about communication.

---

The common thread across all of this is consideration made concrete. Most friction on async teams doesn't come from bad intentions. It comes from people writing for themselves instead of for the reader, and from habits formed in offices where you could just tap someone on the shoulder. You can't tap anyone on the shoulder now. The message has to do that work, and it's worth taking a few extra minutes to make sure it can.
