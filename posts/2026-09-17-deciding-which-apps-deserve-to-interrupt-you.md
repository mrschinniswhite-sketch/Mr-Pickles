Most people treat notification settings as a one-time chore done during app setup, which means they're effectively designed by product managers at software companies whose interests are not yours. Taking an hour to redesign this deliberately is one of the higher-leverage things you can do for sustained attention.

## What a notification policy actually is

A notification policy is a set of rules — made once, applied consistently — that determines what can interrupt you, when, and how loudly. It has three rough tiers: sound (the most expensive, meaning it breaks whatever you're doing), badge (a count sitting on an icon that you check when you choose to), and nothing (the app gets no real estate in your attention at all). Most people have this inverted, handing out sounds generously and reserving "nothing" for apps they forgot to configure.

The goal is not to miss less. It is to make the cost of an interruption proportional to its actual urgency, as defined by you, not by the sender.

## The question to ask about each channel

For every communication channel — messaging apps, email, calendar, project tools, news, social — ask one question: does a message here ever require a response within five minutes? Not "could it," not "sometimes," but does it routinely and legitimately demand that. If the answer is no, it does not earn a sound. For most people working remotely, the honest list of channels that clear this bar is very short: maybe a primary phone call, maybe one specific messaging thread with a partner or dependent. That's often it.

The five-minute test is useful because it forces you to confront the difference between urgency and anxiety. Email feels urgent. It almost never is. A Slack message from a colleague feels urgent because someone is waiting. But "someone is waiting" is not the same as "something bad happens in five minutes if I don't respond." Badge it. Let them wait eight minutes.

## Badges are not neutral

It's tempting to treat badges as a harmless compromise — no sound, but you'll see it when you look. The problem is that badges on apps you open frequently are a constant low-grade pull. You glance at your phone and see 14 on the email icon and something in your brain registers "unresolved," even if you don't open it. Multiply this across a dozen apps and you have an ambient layer of unfinished business following you around.

The solution is to use badges only on channels where the count is actually informative and you have a defined time to process them. If you check a particular tool twice a day at scheduled times, a badge there is useful context before you open it. If you check email compulsively whenever you see the number, the badge is making that worse, not helping you triage.

## Separating "do not disturb" from your base policy

A notification policy isn't just about standing settings — it needs a time dimension. Most devices have a focus or do-not-disturb mode that can suppress everything, or everything except a defined allowlist. This is where you put actual urgency back in. During deep work hours, the base policy might already be fairly quiet, but a DND schedule ensures that even the modest interruptions you've allowed (that badge, that occasional sound) are completely paused.

The allowlist approach is underused. Rather than "allow calls from everyone" or "allow calls from nobody," most systems let you specify that calls or messages from certain contacts bypass DND. That's the right model: build a small allowlist of people who genuinely need to reach you instantly, and let DND be otherwise strict.

## Try this

Open your phone's notification settings and go app by app through everything that currently has sound enabled. For each one, ask the five-minute test: does this channel routinely require a response within five minutes. If not, turn the sound off — not the notification, just the sound. Do not change anything else yet. Do this for every app, which should take under fifteen minutes. Run it for a week unchanged. Most people discover that nothing bad happened, and that the silence is recoverable.

## The policy as a maintained document

Notification settings drift. You install something new, accept defaults, and suddenly there's another source of sounds you didn't consciously authorize. It's worth keeping a short note — literally a few lines in whatever you use for personal documentation — that records your rules. Something like: "sound: emergency contacts only. Badge: calendar, primary messaging. Nothing: everything else." When you install something new, you refer to the policy instead of accepting whatever the app asks for. It takes thirty seconds and it keeps the design intentional rather than accumulated.

The underlying idea is simple: your attention is the resource, and right now most of the apps on your devices are configured to spend it freely. You can change that.
