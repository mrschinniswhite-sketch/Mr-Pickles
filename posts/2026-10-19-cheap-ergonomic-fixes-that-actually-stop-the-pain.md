Most ergonomic suffering is a positioning problem, not an equipment problem. A few small adjustments that cost nothing or almost nothing will do more for your back and wrists than a $1,500 chair ever could, and the expensive gear tends to sell because it's visible and giftable, not because it outperforms the cheap fixes by any meaningful margin.

## The three things that actually move the needle

**Monitor height.** Your screen should sit at roughly eye level so your head isn't dropped forward. A dropped head — even two or three inches — puts a significant load on your neck and upper back for hours at a time. The fix costs you a stack of books, a ream of printer paper, or a $15 monitor riser. That's it. If you use a laptop, a separate keyboard and mouse become necessary the moment you raise the screen, but that's still a sub-$40 problem if you buy decent used peripherals or a basic wired set.

**Chair height and foot support.** The goal is a roughly 90-degree angle at your hips and knees, with your feet flat on something. Most people's chairs are too low, and almost no one's feet are properly supported unless they're unusually tall. A folded moving blanket, a small wooden step stool, or an actual footrest (they run $20–$30) handles this. This single change reduces lower-back compression more reliably than lumbar pillows, which tend to slip and become decoration within a week.

**Taking your hands off the keyboard.** Not a product — a behavior. Keeping your wrists in constant contact with a hard desk edge while typing creates sustained pressure on the carpal tunnel. Lifting your wrists slightly so they float, and resting only during actual pauses, costs nothing and removes one of the most common sources of wrist and forearm pain. A wrist rest can help some people during pauses, but it turns harmful the moment you're actively typing on top of it, which is how most people use it.

## What the expensive stuff is actually doing

A premium ergonomic chair is mostly selling you adjustability — the ability to fine-tune things precisely. If you already sit with reasonable posture and have your monitor and feet sorted, the marginal gain is small. The same is true for split keyboards, elaborate monitor arms, and anti-fatigue mats for seated work. They're not useless, but they're solutions to problems you probably don't have yet, or problems that could be solved more directly for less money.

Standing desks are a genuine tool for some people, particularly those with certain lower-back conditions, but the evidence that standing all day is better than sitting all day is pretty thin. Alternating between positions has value. Spending $800 on a motorized desk to stand for 20 minutes twice a day is a lot of money for that benefit.

## What people miss because it isn't a product

The most effective ergonomic intervention doesn't exist in a catalog. It's movement frequency — getting up or meaningfully shifting your position every 45 to 60 minutes. Static loading (holding any position without moving) is what accumulates into pain, regardless of how perfect the position is. A $4 kitchen timer does this job. So does any simple reminder tool you already own.

Lighting also goes unaddressed because it doesn't hurt in an obvious, localized way. Eye strain from a screen that's either much brighter or much dimmer than the ambient light around it contributes to headaches and fatigue that people attribute to other causes. Matching your screen brightness to your room is free.

## Try this

Set a timer for 12 minutes. Stand up, and look at your current setup from the side — either get someone to photograph you sitting at your desk in your normal working position, or prop up a phone to do it. Check three things: where your eye line falls relative to the top of your screen, whether your feet are actually flat on something, and what your wrist position looks like during typing. You will almost certainly find at least one of these is meaningfully off. Fix whichever one is worst before you sit back down. One stack of books or one folded blanket, placed now, not ordered next week.

## The actual hierarchy

Positioning first, behavior second, cheap props third, expensive gear last if at all. The pain most desk workers experience is almost always a basic positioning and movement problem, and it responds to basic solutions. The expensive equipment market exists partly because people find it easier to buy something than to change a habit, and partly because the cheap fixes feel too simple to be the real answer. They are.
