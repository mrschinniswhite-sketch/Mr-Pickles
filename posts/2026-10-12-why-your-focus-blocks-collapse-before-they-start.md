Most people design their deep work blocks around the moment they sit down, which is already too late. The conditions that determine whether you'll last 90 minutes or bail at 18 were set an hour before you opened the document.

## The real reason you bail early

It's not distraction in the way people usually mean it. You're not failing because your phone buzzed. You're failing because you sat down with no clear idea of what "done" looks like for this block, so your brain — which genuinely cannot tolerate open-ended ambiguity — starts generating escape routes. Checking email feels like work. Refilling your coffee feels like self-care. Both are your nervous system voting to exit an uncomfortable undefined state.

App blockers address the escape routes. They don't address why you needed one.

## The entry problem

Deep work blocks fail at the seams, not the middle. The first 20 minutes are a threshold, and most people cross it without a ritual, without a defined endpoint, and without having cleared the previous cognitive load from whatever they were doing before.

If you came from a meeting, your head is still partly in the meeting. If you came from email, part of you is still composing replies. Switching contexts isn't instantaneous — it's more like changing a tire than flipping a light switch. You need to actually finish the previous state before you can start a new one. Most people skip this and then wonder why they can't concentrate.

A short transition practice — even just five minutes of writing down everything still rattling around from the previous task — acts as a buffer. You're not meditating. You're clearing a cache.

## The problem with vague intentions

"Work on the proposal" is not a plan. It's a category. When you sit down with a category instead of a task, the first thing you'll do is figure out what to actually do, and that low-grade figuring-out is indistinguishable to your brain from the scrolling it's trying to avoid.

Before the block starts, write one sentence that describes the specific output you're after. Not "work on the report" but "finish the methodology section through the data sources paragraph." It can be wrong — you might get there in 40 minutes or it might take two sessions — but having a target changes the internal experience of the work entirely. You have somewhere to go. Momentum becomes possible.

## What your environment is actually doing

The physical setup matters, but not for the reasons productivity culture usually gives. It's not about having a clean desk because clutter is distracting. It's that your environment sends continuous signals about what mode you're in. If you work, browse, eat lunch, and take calls at the same desk in the same chair with the same browser open, your brain has no reliable signal that this particular session is different.

Small, consistent environmental cues — a specific physical object you only put out during deep work, a particular drink, even just closing all windows and reopening only what you need — function as a mode-switch. Over time they become load-bearing. The association does some of the willpower work for you.

This doesn't require a dedicated room or expensive equipment. It requires consistency. The cue has to mean the same thing every time.

## Try this

Before your next intended deep work block, do this in the 10 minutes prior. Write down, on paper or in a plain text file, two things: what you're carrying in from before (unfinished thoughts, open loops, things you're half-worried about), and exactly what you're trying to produce in this session — one sentence, specific enough that you'd know when you'd hit it. Then change one physical thing about your space that you'll change back when the block ends. It doesn't matter what it is. That's it. Don't start the block until you've done all three.

This isn't a magic trick. But it separates the transition from the work itself, which is the thing most people never do.

## The 20-minute mark is a symptom

If you consistently lose focus around the same time, it's worth asking what's happening at that moment — not to blame yourself, but to get diagnostic about it. Usually it's one of three things: you've run out of obvious next steps, you've hit a hard part and your brain is proposing an exit, or you never fully arrived in the first place.

The fix for each of those is different. But none of them is solved by locking your phone in another room. That just removes one door while the underlying pressure looks for another one.
