Most people let app developers decide how often they get interrupted. That is a strange thing to outsource. A notification policy is just a written set of rules you make once and apply consistently — it takes about twenty minutes to build and saves a lot of ambient anxiety.

## What a notification policy actually is

It is not "turn off all notifications" or "be more mindful about your phone." Those are aspirations, not systems. A policy is a small document — even a list in a notes app — that assigns every notification source on your devices to one of three tiers: sound, badge, or nothing. Sound means it can interrupt you in real time. Badge means it marks itself as waiting but stays quiet. Nothing means it exists only when you go looking for it.

The goal is not silence for its own sake. Some interruptions are legitimate. The policy just makes you the person who decided which ones.

## The three-tier logic

**Sound** should be reserved for things where a ten-minute delay has real consequences. Most people land on: phone calls from people they know, a small set of contacts who might have genuine emergencies, and time-sensitive calendar alerts they actually need. That is usually a shorter list than expected.

**Badge** is for things that are real and require a response, but not necessarily right now. Work messages, email from actual humans (not newsletters, not automated receipts), and anything where same-day is fast enough. Badges let you batch your responses instead of dripping attention all day. They also have a cost — badge counts accumulate and create a kind of visual pressure — so keep this tier honest.

**Nothing** is for everything else. Promotional emails, social media activity, news alerts, app update nudges, weather, step counts, most marketing. These get checked when you decide to check them, or not at all. The nothing tier is also where speculative interruptions go: notifications you kept "just in case" but have never once acted on urgently.

## Where people go wrong

The most common mistake is treating notification settings as a one-time setup. Developers push new notification types with updates, apps default to maximum visibility, and within a few months the policy has eroded. Calendar a short review — twice a year is probably enough — where you go through settings and reassign anything that has drifted up a tier without earning it.

The second mistake is building a policy only for the phone and forgetting the laptop. Desktop notifications are often worse, because they interrupt focused work rather than just ambient phone-checking. The same three tiers apply.

The third is the exception creep. "I'll keep Slack sounds on just during work hours" becomes "I'll keep them on during work hours and just check quickly at night" and then nothing has changed. If something belongs in the badge tier, put it there cleanly. Half-measures tend to collapse.

## How to handle the social expectations problem

Some people worry that muting contacts or communication tools will make them seem unresponsive. The fix is to be explicit about your response windows rather than pretending you are always reachable. If colleagues or clients know you respond to messages within a few hours during working days, a badge serves that expectation fine. The anxiety is usually about implicit expectations — what you imagine people assume — rather than any stated requirement. When in doubt, ask rather than guess.

## Try this

Open your phone's notification settings right now and look at the first five apps in the list. For each one, write down — literally write it, even in a scratch note — which tier it belongs in and why. Not what tier it is currently set to: what tier it has actually earned based on real consequences. If you cannot quickly name a specific time when a sound alert from that app mattered, it probably does not belong in the sound tier. Do not change anything yet. Just notice the gap between the current setup and the one you would design deliberately. Most people find at least two or three sources sitting in the sound tier with no real justification. That gap is the policy.

---

The point is not to be unreachable or to perform ascetic focus. It is to be the one making the call. Every sound your devices make is a small claim on your attention; a policy just means you pre-decided which claims are worth honoring, instead of saying yes to all of them by default.
