Most people set up a two-hour deep work block, sit down, and are mentally somewhere else by minute twelve. The block fails not because of notifications or weak willpower, but because of how the session was designed before it started.

## The runway problem

Deep focus doesn't start when you open the document. It starts earlier, and if you skip the approach, you spend the first stretch of your block just circling. The common version of this looks like: finish a meeting or a loose task, immediately open the "focus" session, stare at the work, feel vaguely unsettled, check something small "just to clear it," and now you're in a browser tab. What happened isn't distraction failure. It's that your brain had no idea what it was supposed to be doing or why, so it defaulted to something that felt like forward motion.

The fix here is a written, specific starting condition. Not "work on the report" but "write the methodology section, starting with the second paragraph because the opening is already drafted." Specificity removes the micro-decision that derails everything. You sit down and there is only one next move.

## Unresolved loops burning background cycles

Open loops — the email you didn't send, the thing you said you'd check on, the vague anxiety about a conversation — don't pause when you declare a focus block. They keep running. Cognitive load research doesn't need a citation here; anyone who has tried to draft something complex while mentally rehashing an unfinished argument already knows this.

The underrated practice is a two-minute written brain dump before the block begins. Not a task list. A capture of whatever is unresolved and sitting in working memory: half-formed worries, things you're tracking mentally, decisions pending. Write them down somewhere you trust you'll see them later. The goal is to externalize the loops so your brain stops holding them in RAM. This sounds trivial. It is not trivial. It's probably the single highest-leverage two minutes in a work session.

## Wrong block length for the actual task

There's a habit of treating "deep work block" as a uniform thing, like all focused work takes the same shape. It doesn't. Some work requires long, unbroken stretches for the difficulty to compound into insight. Other work is genuinely better in shorter, more intense sessions — particularly tasks that involve judgment or editing, where fatigue degrades quality faster than time helps it.

If your blocks keep collapsing, consider whether you're scheduling them for the right duration. A ninety-minute block for a task that realistically requires forty-five minutes of genuine effort produces fifty minutes of drift and guilt. Try matching the block length to an honest estimate of the work's natural unit, not to some ideal from a productivity book.

## The re-entry failure

The block doesn't just fail once. It fails, you catch yourself, and then the question is whether you can get back in. Most people can't, because re-entry requires the same conditions as the original entry: a specific starting point, low open loops, and a moment of deliberate transition.

If you've broken focus — genuinely broken it, not just paused — the fastest path back is a one-sentence note of exactly where you left off and what you were about to do next. Write it before you do anything else, even before you deal with what interrupted you. This note is your re-entry ramp. Without it, every return to the work starts from scratch.

## Try this

Before your next focus block — not during it, before — take eight minutes to do three things in sequence. First, write a single sentence that describes exactly what you're trying to produce in the session and where you're starting (not the topic, the specific starting point). Second, spend two minutes writing down everything that is currently unresolved or vaguely worrying you, in any form, without organizing it. Third, read the starting-point sentence one more time before you open anything. That's the whole exercise. Do it the same way three sessions in a row and see whether the first twenty minutes feel different.

The honest version of deep work is less about defending a block from the outside and more about arriving at it in a state where your brain has something clear to do and nothing urgent competing for the same circuits. App blockers address a symptom. The runway, the brain dump, and the specific starting condition address the structure underneath it.
